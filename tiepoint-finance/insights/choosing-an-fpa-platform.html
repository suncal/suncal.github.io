Choosing an FP&A Platform: Datarails, Cube, Vena, Planful and Adaptive from Workday

A neutral, criteria-based way to compare the mid-market FP&A platforms most finance teams shortlist, with a short note on Anaplan and Pigment, the questions to ask in demos, and the selection mistakes that cost the most.

Key takeaways

  • "Excel-native" and "web-native" describe where the model logic lives and who can change it, not whether you can export to Excel. Decide which you want before you watch a demo.
  • Consolidation depth varies more than the marketing suggests. Test eliminations, partly owned entities, CTA and multiple currencies with your own data, not the vendor's.
  • Integration is where implementations slow down. Ask exactly how each source connects (native API, warehouse, file, partner connector) and who maintains it.
  • Implementation effort is driven by your entities, mappings, data quality and decisions far more than by the product.
  • Score platforms against weighted criteria written before the demos, and make every vendor run the same scripted scenario on your data.

Who this is for: CFOs, FP&A leaders and finance systems managers running a platform selection, or reconsidering one that has not delivered.

No vendor sponsored or reviewed this guide. Descriptions below are taken from each vendor's own website or documentation, as cited, and reflect what those sources said when we checked them in November 2026. Products change quickly: verify current capabilities and pricing directly with each vendor.

Start with the decisions, not the products

Before comparing vendors, answer five questions. They narrow the field more than any feature list.

  1. Where do you want model logic to live? In spreadsheets your analysts own (Excel-centric), or in a governed web model that a smaller group administers (web-centric)?
  2. How much consolidation do you need? Management roll-ups across a few entities, or a consolidation with eliminations, partly owned subsidiaries, multiple currencies and CTA that the controller will rely on?
  3. What is the planning scope? Finance only (P&L, balance sheet, cash), or also workforce, sales capacity, supply chain or other operational plans?
  4. Who will run it after go-live? A dedicated systems person, a finance analyst part-time, or a partner?
  5. What must it connect to? List every source: ERP, CRM, HRIS, payroll, billing, banks, data warehouse.

The criteria that actually separate platforms

1. Excel-native versus web-native

Excel-centric Web-centric
Where logic lives Largely in spreadsheets connected to a central database In the platform's own model (dimensions, formulas, rules)
Who builds Analysts with strong Excel skills Model builders trained in the platform
Strength Fast adoption, familiar formulas, flexible formats Governance, scale, consistent logic across users
Risk Logic scattered across workbooks; version and formula control Dependence on a few trained admins; less flexibility for ad hoc work

Most platforms now offer both a web interface and an Excel connection, so the useful question is: when the model changes, where does the change get made?

2. Consolidation depth

Ask for a demonstration, on your data, of: intercompany matching and eliminations; a partly owned entity (full consolidation with non-controlling interest, not just a percentage applied to the numbers); multiple functional currencies with average, closing and historical rates; CTA calculation; top-side journals with audit trail; and different fiscal calendars. Our guides on consolidation and FX translation explain what correct output looks like.

3. Modeling flexibility

Number and type of dimensions, driver-based logic, scenario and version management, allocations, workforce planning by position, multi-year and rolling forecasts, and how the model handles sparse data (many dimensions where most combinations are empty).

4. Integration approach

Native API connectors, warehouse connections, file-based feeds (SFTP), third-party iPaaS, or custom APIs. Who builds and maintains each connector, how refresh is scheduled, how failures are reported, and whether access to the source is read-only.

5. Typical buyer and implementation effort

We do not repeat vendors' own claims about implementation speed. The drivers we plan around are: number of entities and source systems, chart of accounts complexity, data quality, consolidation requirements, number of reports and models, and how quickly your team makes decisions. Our guide on running an FP&A implementation covers these.

The platforms, from their own descriptions

Datarails

  • Positioning: Datarails describes itself as "a completely Excel-native and AI powered FP&A solution", and its website material says it is best suited to small and medium-sized businesses that use Excel.
  • Consolidation: Its consolidation page describes multi-entity, multi-currency consolidation with built-in intercompany eliminations and currency translation, drill-down to entity data, and a Data Mapper to standardize data from different entities, currencies and file formats.
  • Integrations: It lists connectors including NetSuite, QuickBooks (Online and Desktop), Sage Intacct, Microsoft Business Central, Xero, several SAP products, Sage, Acumatica, Epicor and Oracle, plus custom API and SFTP options. It cites 600+ connected systems (one page's metadata says 200+), so confirm the specific connectors you need.
  • Ask in the demo: how the Excel add-in and the central database divide responsibilities; how a mapping change propagates to existing reports.

Cube

  • Positioning: Cube describes itself as "the AI-powered financial intelligence platform" and an "Agentic FP&A Platform", with Excel and Google Sheets as one of several surfaces alongside its web app, Slack and Teams.
  • Integrations: Cube says it connects through native APIs, warehouse or database connections (such as Snowflake) or secure file transfer, depending on the source; data is mapped once to your chart of accounts and dimensions; syncs run on a schedule or on demand; and connections are read-only where the source supports it, with every sync logged. Its help center documents applying NetSuite's consolidated exchange rates by account.
  • Consolidation: Its site lists Data Consolidation and Financial Close pages. Test multi-entity depth directly.
  • Ask in the demo: how Google Sheets and Excel users stay on the same version; how NCI and CTA are handled.

Vena

  • Positioning: Vena calls itself "The #1 Microsoft-Native FP&A Platform", built around Excel, with integrations to Microsoft 365 and pre-built Power BI dashboards (Vena Insights). It describes CubeFLEX as an OLAP database "built specifically for Excel".
  • Consolidation: Its Financial Consolidation solution describes automated intercompany eliminations, multi-currency translation and CTA, ownership and minority interests, top-side adjustments and system-generated audit history. It also lists account reconciliation and tax provisioning as use cases.
  • Integrations: It lists Salesforce, Microsoft Dynamics 365, Business Central, QuickBooks, ADP, Sage, SAP, Workday and NetSuite.
  • Ask in the demo: how template governance works when many users build in Excel; how consolidation and planning share one model.

Planful

  • Positioning: Planful describes "one financial performance management platform" connecting planning, close, consolidation and reporting, and lists Spotlight for Microsoft 365.
  • Consolidation: Its help center documents a consolidation process that runs from local currency to common, interim and reporting currencies; processes elimination sets; calculates non-controlling interest; and calculates currency translation adjustments and retained earnings.
  • Integrations: Planful's site says "thousands of connectors". Ask which are native and which go through partners.
  • Ask in the demo: how close and consolidation workflows interact with planning versions; what the admin skill set is.

Adaptive from Workday (formerly Workday Adaptive Planning)

  • Positioning: Workday describes Adaptive as a "cloud-based enterprise planning platform" for budgeting, forecasting, reporting, analysis and scenario modeling, and notes that "Adaptive from Workday" is the new name for Workday Adaptive Planning. It describes its Elastic Hypercube Technology as "a multidimensional, in-memory engine", with "click-not-code" modeling.
  • Consolidation: Workday's documentation describes intercompany account and level settings, elimination rules, a consolidation percentage sheet to choose consolidation methods and track minority interest, and journal entries; it advises contacting Workday professional services or an implementation partner to get started.
  • Excel: Workday documents OfficeConnect for working with Excel.
  • Ask in the demo: how workforce and operational models share dimensions with the financial model; how non-Workday HR data is integrated.

A note on enterprise: Anaplan and Pigment

Larger groups planning across finance, supply chain, sales and workforce often also evaluate:

  • Anaplan, which describes itself as a scenario planning and analysis platform, names a Polaris Calculation Engine, and lists supply chain, sales and marketing, HR and workforce and retail planning alongside finance.
  • Pigment, which describes itself as "the AI platform built for real-time business planning", with solutions for finance, supply chain, sales and revenue, HR and ESG teams.

Both are web-centric modeling platforms aimed at connected planning across functions. If your requirement is mainly financial consolidation and management reporting, check whether that is a strength or something you would build.

Questions to ask in every demo

Scenario first. Send each vendor the same anonymized data set and scenario two weeks before the demo: two or three entities, one in a foreign currency, one partly owned, some intercompany, a budget and a month of actuals. Ask them to show:

  1. Loading actuals from your ERP and tying the trial balance to the ledger by entity and month.
  2. A new GL account appearing in the source. What happens? Where does it show up?
  3. Changing the mapping of one account and showing the effect on history.
  4. Intercompany eliminations, with a deliberate mismatch.
  5. The partly owned entity, with NCI.
  6. Translation with average, closing and historical rates, and the CTA.
  7. A late journal posted to a closed month. How is it detected?
  8. A budget version, a reforecast, and a variance report with drill-down to transactions.
  9. A failed refresh. How is the user told?
  10. The audit trail for a changed number.

Integration questions.

  1. For each of our sources, is the connector built and maintained by you, a partner, or us?
  2. Is the service account read-only? What permissions does it need in the source?
  3. What are the refresh options: scheduled, on demand, incremental?
  4. What happens to the data if a source system is upgraded?

Commercial and ownership questions.

  1. What does the license count (users, entities, modules, data volume)?
  2. Which modules are needed for what you demonstrated today?
  3. What does a typical admin do each month, and what skills do they need?
  4. What is included in implementation, and what is excluded?
  5. Can we speak to customers of similar size, entity count and ERP?
  6. How do we get our data and models out if we leave?

Selection pitfalls

  • Watching the vendor's demo data. Every platform looks good on a clean, single-currency sample. Use yours.
  • Treating "has consolidation" as a yes/no. It is a spectrum. Test the specific cases you have.
  • Under-weighting admin effort. The person who maintains the model after go-live decides whether the platform succeeds.
  • Letting the integration be someone else's problem. Ask who fixes a broken connector at 7 a.m. on day 2 of close.
  • Skipping the reconciliation question. Ask every vendor how you will prove the platform agrees with the ledger every month.
  • Buying modules for year three. License what you will implement in the first phase, with expansion terms agreed up front.
  • Pricing without dates. If you collect pricing, record the date and what it covered. We do not publish vendor pricing here because it changes and is usually quote-based.
