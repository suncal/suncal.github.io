Running a Successful FP&A Implementation

The four-phase TiePoint Method, who does what, the sign-off gates between phases, what really drives the timeline, the twelve most common reasons implementations stall, and the discovery questionnaire we start with.

Key takeaways

  • An FP&A implementation is a finance data project first and a software project second. Most delays come from data, mapping and decisions, not from the platform.
  • Use gated phases with written sign-off. Each gate answers one question, and nothing moves forward until it is answered.
  • Reconcile before you build reports. Dashboards built on unproven data get rebuilt.
  • The timeline is driven by entities, sources, mapping complexity, consolidation needs, report count and decision speed. Know where you sit on each before you commit to a date.
  • Most stalls are predictable. Each of the twelve below has a specific prevention.

Who this is for: CFOs, controllers and FP&A leaders about to implement (or rescue) a planning and reporting platform such as Datarails, Cube, Vena, Planful or Adaptive from Workday, and the finance systems managers who will own it afterwards.

The TiePoint Method: four phases, four gates

Phase Question it answers Main outputs Gate
1. Discover What exactly are we building, from which data, for whom? Systems and entity inventory; mapping plan; dashboard and report specifications; integration design; risks; fixed quote Client signs off scope, specifications and plan
2. Connect & Map Is every source connected and every account, entity, currency and dimension mapped into one structure? Read-only connections; group chart of accounts and mapping tables; dimension mappings; rate tables; consolidation rules Client confirms mapping tables and structures
3. Validate / Tie-out Does the loaded data agree with the general ledger? Tie-out workbook; difference matrix; intercompany and FX checks; root-cause log; evidence pack Five Tie-out Checks pass for every entity and month in scope; controller signs off
4. Deliver & Handover Can the team use it, trust it and run it without us? Dashboards and board packs; training; written month-end runbook; troubleshooting notes Reports accepted; team runs one close with the runbook

The Five Tie-out Checks at gate 3 are: every GL account mapped exactly once; totals tie to the trial balance per entity per month; intercompany nets to zero after eliminations; correct FX rates and periods; large movements explained before release. Our tie-out playbook explains each one.

Why the gates matter

Gates stop the most expensive kind of rework: building on something that later changes. A report built before the mapping is signed off will be rebuilt when the mapping changes. A dashboard released before tie-out will be questioned in its first board meeting, and trust, once lost, is slow to return. A gate is cheap: a short review and a signature.

Roles and RACI

Roles on the client side

  • Executive sponsor (usually the CFO): owns the outcome, breaks ties, protects time.
  • Project lead (controller, FP&A lead or finance systems manager): day-to-day decisions and coordination.
  • Data owners: one per source system and per entity; answer questions about accounts, dimensions and postings.
  • Report owners: the people who will use each report and accept it.
  • IT / security: creates integration identities, approves access, network and security review.
  • Future platform administrator: the person who will run it after handover; involved from day one.

Two rules make the RACI work: only one "A" per row, and the client remains accountable for decisions about its own accounting.

What drives the timeline

We plan most engagements at roughly six to ten weeks from the end of discovery, but the honest answer to "how long?" is "it depends on these six things", and discovery measures each one:

Driver Shorter Longer
Entities One to a few, same ERP Many entities, several ERPs or ledgers
Source systems ERP only, maintained native connector ERP plus CRM, HRIS, payroll, billing, files
Chart of accounts Shared CoA across entities Different local CoAs, conditional mapping
Consolidation Wholly owned, one currency, little intercompany Partly owned entities, several currencies, heavy intercompany
Reports and models A focused set of management reports Many report packs, driver-based planning, workforce models
Decision speed Named owners who answer within a day or two Owners unclear; decisions waiting for meetings

The last driver is usually the largest. A project that waits a week for each mapping decision can double in length with no change in technical scope.

The twelve most common reasons implementations stall, and how to prevent each

  1. Access arrives late. Integration identities, roles and approvals take weeks in some organizations. Prevent: request access in the first week of discovery, with the exact roles and permissions listed. Track it as a dependency with an owner.

  2. No one owns the data. Questions about accounts or postings go unanswered because nobody knows whose they are. Prevent: name a data owner per source and per entity before Connect & Map begins.

  3. Mapping by committee. Group CoA debates reopen every week. Prevent: design from the required outputs, decide a single owner for the group CoA, and freeze the structure at the Connect & Map gate. Later changes go through change control.

  4. The ledger has known problems nobody mentioned. Misposted accounts, unreconciled intercompany, stale balances. Prevent: ask directly in discovery, and run a trial tie-out on one entity early. Ledger clean-up is the client's accounting work and can run in parallel.

  5. Reports designed before data is proven. Dashboards are built, then rebuilt after tie-out changes the numbers. Prevent: hold report build until the tie-out gate passes. Design and mock-ups can happen earlier; building cannot.

  6. Intercompany does not match. Eliminations leave residuals no one can explain. Prevent: require a partner dimension on all intercompany lines, agree an intercompany policy (cut-off, which side leads), and reconcile pairs before consolidation testing.

  7. FX policy is undocumented. The platform's translated numbers differ from the ERP's, and nobody can say which is right. Prevent: document rate source, average method and rate types in discovery. Import the ERP's rates where possible.

  8. Scope creep. New reports, entities or models are added mid-build. Prevent: a written scope at the discovery gate, and a simple change request with impact on time and cost. Park extras for a phase two.

  9. The future admin is not involved. Handover happens to someone who has never seen the build. Prevent: make the admin part of the project from week one, building alongside the implementation team.

  10. License or module surprises. A needed feature turns out to be in a module that was not purchased. Prevent: confirm license status, modules and user counts during discovery, against the specifications.

  11. Close season collides with the project. The team disappears for two weeks during month-end. Prevent: plan the project calendar around the close calendar. Schedule tie-out work for after each close, when fresh data is available.

  12. No acceptance criteria. Reports are "nearly done" for weeks because nobody defined done. Prevent: each report specification includes acceptance criteria (data, layout, drill-down, refresh) and a named report owner who accepts it.

Discovery questionnaire

We use a version of this at the start of every discovery. It is also a useful self-assessment before talking to any implementer.

Systems

  1. Which ERP or ERPs hold the general ledger for each entity? Version and edition (for example NetSuite OneWorld, Business Central online or on-premises, SAP S/4HANA cloud or on-premises)?
  2. Which other sources feed reporting today: CRM, HRIS, payroll, billing, banks, data warehouse, spreadsheets?
  3. Which FP&A or BI platform has been chosen, and which modules are licensed?
  4. Are there existing integrations, saved searches or scheduled exports that feed reporting today? Who maintains them?

Entities and structure

  1. List the legal entities in scope, with country, functional currency, ERP and fiscal year-end.
  2. What is the ownership structure? Any partly owned entities, joint ventures or entities acquired or disposed of in the periods in scope?
  3. Do entities share one chart of accounts or have local charts? How many accounts in each?
  4. Which dimensions are used (departments, classes, locations, projects, cost centers, custom segments), and are they consistent across entities?

Currencies

  1. Which currencies are involved, and what is the presentation currency?
  2. What is the FX policy: rate source, closing rate definition, average method, historical rates for equity?
  3. Are rates maintained in the ERP, and are they the ones the group reports with?

Intercompany

  1. What intercompany flows exist: goods, services, management fees, cost recharges, loans, interest, dividends?
  2. How are intercompany transactions identified in the ledger (accounts, partner field, both)?
  3. How and when are intercompany balances reconciled today? What residual differences are typical?

Reports

  1. Which reports must the platform produce: management P&L, balance sheet, cash flow, budget versus actual, KPI scorecards, board pack?
  2. Who reads each report, how often, and what decisions does it support?
  3. What drill-down is needed: to account, department, transaction?
  4. Which current reports will be retired?

Close calendar

  1. What is the close calendar by entity (working day by which each entity closes)?
  2. When are periods locked? How common are post-close adjustments?
  3. When must management and board reports be available?

Data owners

  1. Who owns each source system and each entity's ledger?
  2. Who will approve the group chart of accounts and mapping?
  3. Who will sign off the tie-out?
  4. Who will administer the platform after go-live?

Planning scope

  1. Which plans are in scope: annual budget, rolling forecast, scenarios, workforce, capex, cash?
  2. What drivers matter (headcount, price and volume, utilization, pipeline)?
  3. Who contributes to the plan, and what approval workflow is needed?

License status and constraints

  1. Is the platform contract signed? Start date, modules, user types and counts?
  2. Are there security or compliance requirements for integrations (SSO, MFA, IP restrictions, data residency, vendor security review)?
  3. Are there hard deadlines (board meeting, budget cycle, audit, system migration)?

A typical plan on a page

Illustrative example. Durations depend on the drivers above.

Weeks Phase Main activities
1–2 Discover Interviews, systems inventory, sample data, trial tie-out on one entity, specifications, fixed quote
3–5 Connect & Map Connections, group CoA, mapping tables, rate tables, consolidation rules; admin builds alongside
5–7 Validate / Tie-out Load closed months, run the Five Tie-out Checks, resolve differences, evidence pack, sign-off
7–10 Deliver & Handover Build and accept reports; role-based training; runbook; client runs a close with the runbook

Phases overlap at the edges (tie-out on early entities can begin while later entities are still being mapped), but no report is released before its entities pass tie-out.
